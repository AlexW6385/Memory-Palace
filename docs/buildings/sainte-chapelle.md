## 1. The real example

- **What:** the palace chapel of the kings of France in the Palais de la Cité, built to house the relics of the Passion — above all the Crown of Thorns — that Louis IX had bought in 1239 for 135,000 livres; the chapel itself cost 40,000. [stated: european-traveler]
- **Dates:** built in about six years, 1242–1248, consecrated in 1248; Rayonnant Gothic. Restored in the mid-19th century by Félix Duban, Jean-Baptiste Lassus and the young Viollet-le-Duc. [stated: reeddesign; european-traveler; europeforvisitors]
- **State shown:** as consecrated in 1248. Not shown: the Flamboyant west rose (about a century and a half later — we show a Rayonnant rose in its place [inferred]), the 19th-century spire (a spire of the same kind is shown [inferred]).

## 2. Type and typology

A two-storey palace chapel (*Sainte-Chapelle* type): a low lower chapel for the household, and over it the upper chapel for the king and the relics — a single vessel without aisles, four bays and a seven-sided apse, whose walls are almost entirely glass between slender piers; the buttresses outside take the thrust of the vaults, so that inside the vault seems to float above the windows. [stated: europeforvisitors; european-traveler]

## 3. Orientation and site

- Apse to the east, entrance by a two-storey porch on the west. [stated: type; standard]
- In the courtyard of the royal palace on the Île de la Cité, by the Seine. In our world: in a new region, Francia, north of the Levant over a pass; the chapel by the river among palace buildings and a town of timber-framed houses (docs/world.md). [stated: setting; position inferred]

## 5. Section and structure

Ashlar limestone; quadripartite rib vaults on wall shafts; the thrust carried by deep buttresses (no flyers), with iron tie-chains hidden in the masonry (standard Rayonnant practice) [inferred]; a steep timber roof covered in lead; the lower chapel's vaults carry the upper floor. [stated: buttresses — europeforvisitors]

## 6. Sequence of spaces and use

1. **Courtyard of the palace** and the lower porch. [type]
2. **Lower chapel** (dedicated to the Virgin; the parish church of the palace household): low, dense with painted columns. [stated: europeforvisitors]
3. **Upper porch** (from the palace) → **upper chapel** (the king, the court, the relics): the cage of glass; the relics on the tribune in the apse. [stated]

## 7. Materials and finishes

- Limestone (Paris lutetian) [inferred: local building stone]; lead roof [inferred].
- Lower chapel: vault deep blue with gold fleurs-de-lis; columns painted with gold fleurs-de-lis on blue (France) and gold castles on red (Castile, for Blanche of Castile, Louis's mother). [stated: european-traveler; europeforvisitors — the present colour is the 19th-century restoration following medieval designs]
- Upper chapel: vault blue with gold stars; shafts and dado painted and gilded; the glass deep red and blue. [stated: europeforvisitors (stars); colours of glass general]

## 8. Decorative programme (L3)

The fifteen windows (Genesis to the arrival of the relics in Paris, 1,113 scenes); the apostles; the quatrefoil medallions of the dado (42 martyrs, 19th-century); 100 capitals.

## 9. Lighting

Upper chapel: almost all wall is glass — coloured light, dominated by red and blue, from every side; the region's afternoon sun throws coloured patches across the floor. Lower chapel: dim, small windows. [stated: windows; light inferred]

## 10. Surroundings

The Palais de la Cité (towers with conical roofs, the great hall), a town of timber-framed houses with steep gables, the Seine with its willows; meadows, orchards and vineyards on the hills around Paris; elms, oaks, poplars. [inferred: setting] **Built (L2):** the courtyard and its stair, a few palace towers, the town (`src/world/features/`). *L4: Notre-Dame (under construction in 1248), the Grand-Salle.*

**In the city (2026-10-28):** the courtyard stands in the Francia district of the compact city, among blocks of timber-framed houses with elms, oaks and apple orchards in their gardens; the Seine is not shown.

## 11. Music

Paris in the 1240s: the polyphony of the Notre-Dame school — Léonin and Pérotin — organum: a plainchant tenor held in long notes while one to three upper voices move above it in the rhythmic modes (long–short patterns in triple time); plainchant alone in the daily office. [general musicology, e.g. R. Hoppin, *Medieval Music* (1978)] Our synthesis (`src/buildings/sainte-chapelle/music.js`): in the upper chapel a held tenor (on D) with an upper voice moving in the first rhythmic mode in a resonant room; in the lower chapel plainchant alone; outside, a vielle-like bowed drone and bells.
